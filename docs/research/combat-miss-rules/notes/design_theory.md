## 1. Input vs output randomness, and "fortune in the middle" vs "fortune at the end"

### Takeaway
Two vocabularies, one from board games and one from RPG theory, make the same point. Randomness that resolves **before** a player commits (input randomness / fortune in the middle) leaves room for a decision about what to do with the result. Randomness that resolves **after** commitment, with nothing to do in response (output randomness / fortune at the end), is where "I did nothing this turn" comes from. Geoff Engelstein is credited with the board-game terms. Ron Edwards coined the RPG terms on the Forge and says he first stated the idea in his review of *Hero Wars*. A binary d20 hit/miss with no follow-up decision is the textbook case of output randomness / fortune at the end.

### Cited Findings
**Input vs output randomness**
- [Established] Definitions: input randomness resolves before the player decides, and the player then works with what it produced. Example: drawing a Carcassonne tile, then choosing where to place it. Output randomness is the reverse: "first the player makes a decision, and then a random event happens that impacts the decision", with dice rolled to resolve an attack as the usual example. Poker contains both. — [Skeleton Code Machine, "Input-Output Randomness (Part 1)"](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/input-output-randomness-part-1); [Skeleton Code Machine, Part 2](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/input-output-randomness-part-2); [Level 99 Games design guidelines §3.5.1–3.5.2](https://www.level99games.com/blogs/guidelines/3-5-1-3-5-2-input-output-randomness); [BGG thread "Input vs Output randomness"](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/1294990/input-vs-output-randomness). These pages came back together in one search and the excerpts did not say which page held which example.
- [Established] Attribution: the terms are popularized by and credited to Geoff Engelstein. In a BoardGameGeek thread on *Ludology* Episode 34 ("The Good, The Bad, and The Random"), designer Lewis Pulsipher credits Engelstein with coining "output randomness". — [BGG: Ludology Episode 34 thread](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/814097/ludology-episode-34-the-good-the-bad-and-the-rando)
- [Designer opinion] Engelstein's GDC 2018 Board Game Design Day talk, "White, Brown, and Pink: The Flavors of Tabletop Game Randomness", sorts randomness into "flavors": white noise, where the last result predicts nothing about the next (the most common kind in games), plus brown and pink noise, and input vs output randomness. — [GDC Vault session listing](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024920/contactUs); [Engelstein GDC 2018 slides (PDF)](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/Engelstein_Geoffrey_White_Brown_Pink.pdf)
- [Designer opinion] Engelstein's position, as an academic paper summarizes it: input randomness supports strategy because players can assess the information and plan, while output randomness undercuts strategic planning. The same paper quotes Keith Burgun agreeing that "input randomness is definitely better than output randomness". The paper itself studies how input vs output randomness affects satisfaction in collectible card games. — [arXiv 2107.08437, "Effect of Input-output Randomness on Gameplay Satisfaction in Collectable Card Games"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08437)
- [Designer opinion] Keith Burgun treats input/output as a **spectrum** set by how much game time the player has to respond: "The less time a player has to respond to the random event, the more Burgun would describe the random event as output random." Random map generation sits at the input end and a combat die roll is clearly output. — [Kind Fortress, "Design Patterns: Random Loops" (2018)](https://www.kindfortress.com/2018/10/03/design-patterns-random-loops/)
- [Designer opinion] Burgun also says randomness matters in a good game but "not all randomness is equal", and warns that "it's actually pretty easy to design multiplayer strategy games and have them seem to 'work' if everything is just… extremely random." — [Keith Burgun, "Why You Need the Clockwork Game"](http://keithburgun.net/why-you-need-the-clockwork-game/)
- Engelstein & Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design: An Encyclopedia of Mechanisms* (CRC Press). First edition about 2019–2020, 516 pp. Second edition 2022, 626 pp., which has "Chapter 4 Resolution" and "Chapter 6 Uncertainty". Each entry gives how the mechanism works, pros and cons, implementation notes and example games. I could not see the chapter text. — [Routledge, 2nd edition page](https://www.routledge.com/Building-Blocks-of-Tabletop-Game-Design-An-Encyclopedia-of-Mechanisms/Engelstein-Shalev/p/book/9781032015811); [sack.de, 1st edition listing](https://www.sack.de/engelstein-shalev-building-blocks-of-tabletop-game-design/9781138365520)

**Fortune in the middle (FitM) vs fortune at the end (FatE)**
- [Established] Ron Edwards coined both "fortune in the middle" and "fortune at the end" "to describe unexamined practices that had been hanging around the hobby for decades." Justin Alexander (The Alexandrian) describes fortune positioning as where the dice roll falls in resolving an action and what happens before and after it, and FitM as "creating an additional decision point in the middle of resolution". He also says FatE is what most GMs and groups default to. — [The Alexandrian, "Art of Rulings 10: Fortune Positioning"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38570/roleplaying-games/art-of-rulings-10-fortune-positioning)
- [Designer opinion, primary] Edwards on the Forge forums: "I think I articulated it first in my review of Hero Wars." In FatE, everything about the action is settled before the roll and the dice have the final word. In FitM, the roll comes **before** the specific actions, positions and communication of the characters are fully described, and the result is used to establish those details after the fact. — [Forge archive, "What is Fortune in the Middle"](http://indie-rpgs.com/archive/index.php?topic=442.0); [Forge Reference Project](http://indie-rpgs.com/reference/index.php/welcome/topic/13073); [Edwards' Hero Wars review, Forge](http://www.indie-rpgs.com/reviews/16/)
- [Designer opinion, primary] *Hero Wars* example: players "announce only general intent prior to rolling, and that's not intent about the ACTION, but about the entire conflict". The shift is from "I shoot my arrow at him" to "I put him out of action with my arrow shot". After the roll is examined, a player **can spend a Hero Point to bump it**. That is a direct precedent for spending a resource after the roll. — [Forge archive topic 442](http://indie-rpgs.com/archive/index.php?topic=442.0)
- [Designer opinion] In "GNS and Other Matters of Role-playing Theory, Chapter 4", Edwards calls fortune-in-the-middle the main reason Narrativist design can still make excellent use of Fortune (dice) mechanics, and gives *Castle Falkenstein*'s magic system as an example. — [The Forge, GNS Chapter 4](https://www.indie-rpgs.com/articles/5/)
- [Community] Bare-bones FitM: declare what the character tries, roll, then narrate the outcome from the dice, so "the dice are not the final arbiter of what happens in the game's fiction." — [Socratic Design, "How Do Fortune Resolution Mechanics Work?" (2011)](http://socratesrpg.blogspot.com/2011/02/how-do-fortune-resolution-mehcanics.html)
- [Community] Most Forge games use "Fortune in the Middle (with Teeth)" resolution. — [TV Tropes, The Forge](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Creator/TheForge). One wiki notes the term confused people because it mixed up players' mechanical contributions with their narrative ones. — [RPG Museum wiki, "Fortune at the end"](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Fortune_at_the_end)
- [Rules (secondary)] Momentum in the 2d20 system is a modern spend-after-the-roll mechanic. Successes beyond the Difficulty become Momentum, which can be spent at once or banked in a group pool (capped at 6 in *Conan*). Spends include improving the quality of the success, extra damage, an extra action, or +1d20 on a later test. The GM has a mirror pool called Doom, Threat or Heat depending on the game. — [Modiphius, "2d20 Basics"](https://modiphius.net/blogs/news/2d20-basics); [Black Gate, Conan 2d20 report](https://www.blackgate.com/?p=445845); [Modiphius forum, Momentum/Threat example](https://forums.modiphius.com/t/example-to-help-new-game-masters-explain-how-momentum-threat-works/18653)

### Inferences
- Today's single d20 hit/miss with nothing to do afterward is pure output randomness / FatE. The whiff turn is the predictable result of that structure, not bad luck.
- Direction (a), stats choosing the targeted defense, adds a **pre-roll** decision ("which defense do I go after?"). That makes the attack more meaningful, but the result is still fortune at the end, so it does not by itself remove whiff turns.
- Direction (b), partial hits plus momentum, is the one that brings in fortune-in-the-middle traits. Banking momentum and spending it after a roll (as with the Hero Point bump or 2d20 Momentum) gives a decision point after the dice land.
- Other common FitM patterns, all **UNVERIFIED (memory)**: 5e Bardic Inspiration and the Lucky feat, which can be used after seeing the d20 but before the outcome is announced; Battle Master Precision Attack, added after the roll; Blades in the Dark "resistance" rolls; dice-placement board games where you roll first and then assign dice (*Roll for the Galaxy*). Check these against the rulebooks or the SRD before citing them.

### Gaps
- I could not open Engelstein's own text (*GameTek*, *Building Blocks*, the Ludology audio, or the GDC slides) to get his exact definitions in his own words. The definitions above come through secondary pages.
- A search summary attributed a counterpoint to Mark Brown (Game Maker's Toolkit): well-tuned output randomness can improve a game, and badly designed input randomness can hurt it. The excerpt did not tie it to a specific page. **UNVERIFIED.**
- The "Drama / Karma / Fortune" resolution trio that Edwards built on is usually credited to Jonathan Tweet's *Everway* (1995). **UNVERIFIED (memory).**

## 2. Greg Costikyan, *Uncertainty in Games* (MIT Press, 2013)

### Takeaway
Costikyan argues that uncertainty is essential to engagement, and that randomness is only **one** of about eleven sources of uncertainty. Others, such as player unpredictability, hidden information, analytic complexity and performative uncertainty, can carry a game without dice. In this framing a turn that does nothing is a design failure only because the uncertainty resolved into nothing the player can act on. Uncertainty itself is not the problem.

### Cited Findings
- Bibliographic facts: MIT Press, Playful Thinking series (its second volume; Jesper Juul is series co-editor). Hardcover 2013, ISBN 9780262018968. Paperback 2015, ISBN 9780262527538, 152 pp. — [Jesper Juul, "Greg Costikyan's new book, Uncertainty in Games" (2013)](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2013/05/30/greg-costikyan-uncertainty-in-games/); [MIT Press page](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262527538/uncertainty-in-games/); [The Video Game Library entry](https://www.thevideogamelibrary.org/book/uncertainty-in-games)
- [Designer opinion] Core thesis: players need uncertainty to stay engaged, and mastering uncertainty is at the heart of a game's appeal. Examples range from *Super Mario Bros.* and Rock/Paper/Scissors to *Monopoly*, *CityVille*, FPS deathmatch and Chess. — [The Video Game Library](https://www.thevideogamelibrary.org/book/uncertainty-in-games); [Jesper Juul](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2013/05/30/greg-costikyan-uncertainty-in-games/)
- [Established] The taxonomy. Evan Torner, applying it to analog RPGs, writes that Costikyan "identifies eleven forms of uncertainty", naming among them: performative uncertainty, solver's uncertainty, player unpredictability, randomness, analytic complexity, hidden information, and narrative anticipation. — [Torner, "Uncertainty in Analog Role-Playing Games" (ETC Press PDF)](https://press.etc.cmu.edu/file/download/1014/e2bcece6-70ca-4b37-b5f4-ef49e14a35f6); [Analog Game Studies, Torner Part 2 (2014)](https://analoggamestudies.org/2014/09/uncertainty-in-analog-role-playing-games-part-2/)
- Definitions as summarized by later researchers. **Performative uncertainty** comes from physical execution, such as controlling an avatar (Tetris, Guitar Hero, Super Mario Bros.). **Solver's uncertainty** is whether you can solve a puzzle. **Player unpredictability** comes from other players (Rock/Paper/Scissors, multiplayer games). **Hidden information** is what players cannot see (poker hands). — [Power et al., "Measuring Uncertainty in Games: Design and Preliminary Validation" (CHI 2017 LBW, City Univ. open access)](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/21355/1/lbw0607-powerA.pdf); [Liz England, review of *Uncertainty in Games*](https://lizengland.com/blog/review-uncertainty-in-games-by-greg-costikyan/)
- Costikyan's **randomness** category is "uncertainty in outcome of a game due to random number generation as part of the game rules, such as dice rolls or card shuffles". It is one category among the others. — [arXiv 2503.02686, "Seeding for Success: Skill and Stochasticity in Tabletop Games" (2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.02686)
- Sources disagree on the count. Most say eleven, but Power, Cairns & Denisova (2018) refer to nine. A Blinkist summary gives three types ("outcome, information, meaning") that do not match the book. Treat Blinkist as unreliable. — [Power, Cairns & Denisova, "Measuring Player Uncertainty in Digital Games" (2018, White Rose eprints)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/131344/1/PowerCairnsDenisova2018.pdf); [Blinkist summary](https://www.blinkist.com/en/books/uncertainty-in-games-en)
- [Designer opinion via reviewer] A single dice roll depends heavily on chance, while games with many rolls tend to dilute chance with strategic decisions. — [Liz England review](https://lizengland.com/blog/review-uncertainty-in-games-by-greg-costikyan/)
- [Community] The Thoughtful Gamer's review stresses that in Costikyan's frame randomness is only one of many kinds of uncertainty, and that player unpredictability can be more compelling than a dice roll. Reviewers also call the book short. One describes it as "a perhaps too brief look" at the subject. — [The Thoughtful Gamer review (2018)](https://thethoughtfulgamer.com/2018/07/19/uncertainty-in-games-book-review/)
- [Designer opinion] Torner, building on Costikyan for tabletop RPGs, notes that increasing transparency gives players more agency but also more responsibility for the outcome. — [Torner (ETC Press PDF)](https://press.etc.cmu.edu/file/download/1014/e2bcece6-70ca-4b37-b5f4-ef49e14a35f6)
- [Community, secondhand] A blog summary attributes to Costikyan the view that pure-chance games such as Candy Land and Chutes and Ladders "are not games since there are no decisions to make which affect the outcome". This is secondhand only; check it against Costikyan's own text. — [A Medley of Potpourri, "Game" (2020)](https://amedleyofpotpourri.blogspot.com/2020/07/game.html?m=1)

### Inferences
- Costikyan's framing supports keeping dice in the game, since uncertainty is part of the fun. It also supports adding **other** kinds of uncertainty that do not cost a turn. Hidden information (which defense is weak?) and analytic complexity (which target and which defense?) fit direction (a).
- For players aged 8 and up, the "no decisions = not a game" point backs adding at least one real choice per turn, even when the roll goes badly.

- I could not confirm the remaining four categories from a source I could see. My recollection is: development anticipation, schedule uncertainty, uncertainty of perception, semiotic uncertainty. **UNVERIFIED (memory).**
- I found no direct quote from the book on randomness vs player agency. My recollection that Costikyan argues randomness helps weaker players win and eases analysis paralysis is **UNVERIFIED (memory)**.
- Costikyan's earlier essay "I Have No Words & I Must Design" (1994), which defines games through decision-making, is **UNVERIFIED (memory)**. It was not checked.

## 3. "Fail forward" / "failing forward" in RPG design

### Takeaway
"Fail forward" means a failed roll still moves the situation forward, usually as success with a complication or as a new problem. It came out of indie RPG practice, with Forge-era stakes discussions often cited. 13th Age printed it as a named rule ("Fail Forward!"), and the Powered by the Apocalypse games (Apocalypse World, Dungeon World) made it structural: a miss triggers a GM move. **No reliable source names who coined it.** The main criticisms are that the term is borrowed self-help jargon for an old practice, that it makes failure feel less real, that some PbtA authors say the label misdescribes their system, and that partial results add adjudication work for the GM.

### Cited Findings
- [Community] Origin is uncertain. On an RPG.net thread about the term's origins, one poster recalls the first formal discussions on the Forge forums in the early 2000s, around setting stakes. Another guesses it was Robin Laws. Others credit 13th Age with its "biggest surge in popularity" and point to random encounters as an older precursor. All of this is anecdote. — [RPG.net, "The origins of fail forward"](https://forum.rpg.net/index.php?threads/the-origins-of-fail-forward.815557/)
- [Community] The phrase is "adapted from business self-help jargon". This blog suggests Fate Core's "succeed at a cost" as clearer wording. — [Run a Game, "Fail Forward" (2015)](https://www.runagame.net/2015/12/fail-forward.html)
- [Rules (secondary)] 13th Age, "Fail Forward!" (2012 core, p. 48): when failure would stall the action, the GM treats a failed skill check as a near-success or as success with unwanted side effects, so the story keeps moving. A natural 1 still fumbles, "perhaps in a particularly bad way". — [13th Age Archmage Engine SRD (PDF), Pelgrane](https://pelgranepress.com/files/13th_Age/13th_Age_System_Reference_Document.pdf); [13th Age SRD, "Running the Game"](https://www.13thagesrd.com/running-the-game/)
- [Designer opinion] Justin Alexander defines failing forward as describing the mechanical result of failure "as being a success-with-complications in the game world". He presents it as a beginner's middle ground between forbidding retries and wiping out the intended route, and adds that it is "not the be-all or end-all of how to adjudicate failure. There are more advanced or gradated techniques". — [The Alexandrian, "Failure for the Beginning GM"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm)
- [Rules (secondary)] PbtA structure: roll 2d6 + stat. 6 or less is a miss, 7–9 a partial success, 10+ a full success. The GM makes moves in response to missed rolls, among other times. — [StartPlaying, "What is Powered by the Apocalypse"](https://startplaying.games/blog/posts/what-is-powered-by-the-apocalypse); [Designers & Dragons, "The Evolution of the Apocalypse"](https://www.designers-and-dragons.com/2021/11/02/the-evolution-of-the-apocalypse-a-system-history/). Stonetop describes a 7–9 as a "weak hit": partial success or success with a cost. — [PTFO, "How does Stonetop work"](https://ptfo.substack.com/p/how-does-stonetop-work)
- [Rules (secondary)] Dungeon World's Hack and Slash on a 7–9: you deal your damage **and** the enemy makes an attack against you. That is a combat partial hit where something always happens. — [Dungeon World G+ archive (2012)](https://dungeonworld.gplusarchive.online/2012/12/10/the-hack-and-slash-move-says-on-a-79-you-deal-your-damage-to-the-enemy-and-the-enemy-makes-an-attack-against)
- [Designer opinion] The Daily Apocalypse blog argues that calling Apocalypse World "failing forward" misdescribes how its moves work and what makes a miss as productive as a hit. — [The Daily Apocalypse #143, "Beyond Success & Failure: Failing Forward and Moves in Apocalypse World"](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/143-beyond-success-failure-failing-forward-and-moves-in-apocalypse-world)
- [Designer opinion] In a 2024 r/PBtA thread (as relayed in search excerpts), co-designer Vincent Baker clarified that on certain Apocalypse World road-war moves, when you miss, the other side "get[s] the weak hit". The failure is expressed as the opponent's success. — [r/PBtA mirror, Feb 2024](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/PBtA/comments/1aiajb0/aw_help_with_road_war_moves)
- [Community] One criticism is that partial successes put an adjudication burden on the GM (known from the title only; body not read). — [Drolleries, "The GM Burden of Adjudicating Partial Successes"](https://drolleries.substack.com/p/the-gm-burden-of-adjudicating-partial). A related point: a 7–9 should give the player "something real", or it is effectively a miss. — [Dungeon World G+ archive](https://dungeonworld.gplusarchive.online/2012/12/10/the-hack-and-slash-move-says-on-a-79-you-deal-your-damage-to-the-enemy-and-the-enemy-makes-an-attack-against)
- [Community] Old-school (OSR) criticism: "fail forward" is a jargon label for an unremarkable practice, and old-school play keeps real failure. — [OSRPG Talk, "The Old School: No 'fail forward'"](https://osrpgtalk.net/the-old-school-no-fail-forward/)
- [Community] Arguments for it: it fixes cases where "a failure just did nothing" and invites repeated rolling (lockpicks, rituals). The stated goal is a design "where the story advances no matter what the result". — [Paizo forums, "I wish Paizo would embrace Fail Forward game design"](https://paizo.com/threads/rzs7yvdh?I-wish-Paizo-would-embrace-Fail-Forward-game=). A limited house-rule version lets a near miss (fail by 1–2) succeed with a complication. — [EN World, "Success w/ Complication Module in the DMG or Fail Forward in the Basic PDF"](https://www.enworld.org/threads/do-you-use-the-success-w-complication-module-in-the-dmg-or-fail-forward-in-the-basic-pdf.680103/)
- [Designer opinion] GUMSHOE (Robin D. Laws, Pelgrane) applies the same thinking to investigation. Core clues are found without a roll so a bad roll cannot stall the story, and dice remain only for general abilities. Pelgrane itself warns that an automatic clue can feel less earned. — [Evil Hat, "The Whiff Factor: GUMSHOE Edition"](https://evilhat.com/the-whiff-factor-gumshoe-edition/); [Pelgrane, "Giving Out Clues in GUMSHOE" (2016)](https://pelgranepress.com/2016/03/15/giving-out-clues-in-gumshoe/)
- [Community] Blades in the Dark-inspired variant for d20 combat: subtract a failed attack roll from 20 to get the opponent's roll, so a failed attack becomes an enemy hit rather than nothing. That gives three outcomes instead of hit/miss. — [Komodo Games, "Speeding up combat with failure states"](https://komodogames.substack.com/p/speeding-up-combat-with-failure-states)

### Inferences
- In combat, the PbtA / Dungeon World 7–9 band ("you hit, and something happens to you") is the closest model for a partial hit. In a kids' game, the "cost" side may need to be mild, or replaced with "you gain momentum but deal reduced damage", to avoid punishing young players.
- For an AI or automated DM running play-by-post, the "GM burden" criticism matters less if partial outcomes are **mechanically defined** (fixed graze damage, a momentum token) rather than improvised narration.
- John C. Maxwell's self-help book *Failing Forward* is usually dated 2000, but one search summary said 2007. **UNVERIFIED (memory)**; this is a conflict to resolve before citing.

### Gaps
- No primary source establishes who coined "fail forward" or when it entered RPG use. Robin Laws and the Forge are both speculation.
- I could not confirm the wording of the 5e DMG "Success at a Cost" variant. The EN World thread title implies it exists, and my recollection of DMG ch. 8 is **UNVERIFIED (memory)**.
- Burning Wheel's "intent and task" and "let it ride" principles, often named as precursors, could not be sourced.

## 4. Meaningful choice and dominant strategies, applied to choosing which defense to target

### Takeaway
Sid Meier's "a game is a series of interesting decisions" (first said at GDC 1989, the subject of his 2012 GDC talk) gives a direct test: if a hero always targets the same defense, or the choice makes no difference, it is not an interesting decision. Balance theory (David Sirlin's viable options, Ian Schreiber's transitive/intransitive balancing, and the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium of rock-paper-scissors) says multi-defense targeting only works if no option dominates and each option has a counter. D&D 4e, which had four defenses, is the cautionary example: critics say the defenses ended up numerically similar, so the choice felt redundant. Darkest Dungeon's per-enemy resistance tables and Pokémon's type chart show the targeting decision working, at the cost of players having to learn the matchups.

### Cited Findings
- [Designer opinion, established quote] Sid Meier first described games as "a series of interesting decisions" at GDC in 1989, and his GDC 2012 lecture "Interesting Decisions" was built around that line. — [The Escapist, "GDC 2012: Sid Meier Sees 'Interesting Decisions' Even in Rhythm Games"](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/gdc-2012-sid-meier-sees-interesting-decisions-even-in-rhythm-games/)
- [Designer opinion] Meier's test: a player who always picks the first of three options, or whose choices are effectively random, is not making an interesting decision. When evaluating a design, ask what decisions the player is given and whether they are interesting. He granted that rhythm and puzzle games fit the framing awkwardly. — [Game Developer, "GDC 2012: Sid Meier on how to see games as sets of interesting decisions" (Mar 7, 2012)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)
- [Designer opinion] David Sirlin: a multiplayer game is balanced if "a reasonably large number of options available to the player are viable". "The worst thing you can have in a competitive multiplayer game is a dominant move", meaning one strictly better than the alternatives, which usually also lacks a real counter. — [Sirlin, "Balancing Multiplayer Games, Part 2: Viable Options"](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-2-viable-options); [Part 1: Definitions](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-1-definitions)
- [Designer opinion] Ian Schreiber names three ways to balance game objects. **Transitive** means cost vs benefit along a cost curve. **Intransitive** means rock-paper-scissors cycles where each option beats one and loses to another. **"Fruity"** means tuning by playtest when options cannot be compared directly. When all strategies are intransitive, "there will not be a single dominant strategy that invalidates all of the others". — [Ian Schreiber, Game Balance Concepts, "Level 9: Intransitive Mechanics" (2010)](https://gamebalanceconcepts.wordpress.com/2010/09/01/level-9-intransitive-mechanics/); [Hibberd, "Ian Schreiber on Game Balance" (2013 summary)](http://hibberd100.blogspot.com/2013/03/ian-schreiber-on-game-balance.html)
- [Established, game theory] Rock-paper-scissors has no pure-strategy equilibrium. Its mixed Nash equilibrium is playing each option with probability 1/3, and any predictable lean toward one option can be exploited. — [JoVE, "Mixed strategies"](https://app.jove.com/v/17701); [MathIsFun, "Mixed Strategy Nash Equilibrium"](https://mathsisfun.com/sets/game-mixed-strategy-equilibrium.html). A formal paper argues that a maximally balanced, playable game cannot have dominated strategies. — [arXiv 2511.00374, "Different Forms of Imbalance in Strongly Playable Discrete Games I"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.00374). Hobson's-choice research notes that a dominant strategy can become players' only option even when others exist. — [Atlantis Press, "Research on the Hobson's Choice Effect in Games" (CDSD-22)](https://atlantis-press.com/proceedings/cdsd-22/125984884)
- [Rules (secondary)] D&D 4e replaced most saving throws with four defenses: AC, Fortitude, Reflex and Will. The **attacker** always rolls d20 + modifiers against the named defense (powers say e.g. "Int vs. Fortitude"). Each defense = 10 + half level + the higher of two paired ability modifiers + armor/shield and other bonuses. AC/Reflex use Dex or Int, Fortitude uses Str or Con, Will uses Wis or Cha. — [D&D 4e Wiki, "Defense"](https://dnd4.fandom.com/wiki/Defense); [Olde Greybeard, "D&D 4e: Where Are the Saving Throws?"](https://oldegreybeard.substack.com/p/d-and-d-4e-where-are-the-saving-throws)
- [Community criticism] Because 4e defenses scale with level, "all four of the defenses have similar values for any particular monster", so multiple defenses can feel redundant. Monster AC sits a little higher, but AC-targeting attacks tend to carry a higher attack bonus to offset it. — [Necropraxis, "Nalfeshnee hack monsters" (2012)](https://necropraxis.com/2012/03/05/nalfeshnee-hack-monsters/); [EN World, "AC and 4e"](https://www.enworld.org/threads/ac-and-4e.226025/)
- [Community] 5e "target the weak save" tactic: Dex/Con/Wis are often called the strong saves and Str/Int/Cha the weak ones. Spells against weak saves pay off more but there are few of them, Con is "by far the worst" save to target, and **finding** a monster's weak save usually takes metagaming. One Paizo poster suggests a knowledge check that reveals the weakest defense. — [Game Learning Society, spell DC guide](https://www.gameslearningsociety.org/wiki/what-does-spell-dc-mean/); [Paizo forums, "Math stacked against spellcasters"](https://paizo.com/threads/rzs42oxf&page=2); [EN World, "Weak Saving Throws"](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=6325554)
- [Community / wiki] Darkest Dungeon gives each enemy its own resistance profile, which makes targeting the core decision. Gargoyles have huge PROT and bleed resistance but are weak to blight, so the wiki suggests Plague Doctor, Shieldbreaker or Grave Robber. Ruins skeletons resist bleed and are weak to blight. Stun resistance ranges from 10% to 200% by enemy. The Sleeper has 70% blight vs 140% stun/bleed resistance. Some enemies (Malignant Growth) also target the **party's** lowest resistance. — [Darkest Dungeon Wiki, Gargoyle](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Gargoyle); [Ruins](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Ruins); [Malignant Growth](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Malignant_Growth); [The Sleeper](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/The_Sleeper)
- [Community, low-quality sources] Pokémon: 18 types and 324 matchups. Super-effective hits deal 2x, not-very-effective 0.5x. Each Pokémon has one or two types and each move has one. Every added type means more matchups to memorize (Fairy reshaped competitive play by countering Dragon). The pages found were basic guides and SEO filler; verify before citing. — [Beebom, Pokémon type chart](https://beebom.com/pokemon-type-chart/amp/); [Bill's Archive, type chart](https://billsarchive.com/articles/pokemon-type-chart.html)
- [Community] Criticism of 5e (2024) weapon masteries: most "don't have any disadvantage to not using the property, so players always use them", and they "lack turn-by-turn decision making". Graze is a passive rider, not a choice. — [D&D Beyond forums, "Weapon masteries lack turn-by-turn decision making"](https://www.dndbeyond.com/forums/dungeons-dragons-discussion/unearthed-arcana/182338-weapon-masteries-lack-turn-by-turn-decision-making); [D&D Beyond forums, "Weapon Masteries revision"](https://www.dndbeyond.com/forums/dungeons-dragons-discussion/unearthed-arcana/177709-weapon-masteries-revision)

### Inferences
- Direction (a) passes Meier's test only if foes' defenses **actually differ** enough to change the best choice. 4e shows that if every defense lands within a point or two, the decision disappears. Bestiary data should give each foe a clearly weak and a clearly strong defense, as Darkest Dungeon does.
- If a hero's stats alone pick the defense, the choice moves from the turn to character building (a transitive, build-time decision). To keep a decision **each turn**, heroes need at least two attack options that target different defenses, and foes need visible or discoverable weaknesses. The Paizo "knowledge check reveals weakest defense" idea, or showing a weakness icon to kids, deals with the metagaming problem.
- For ages 8 and up, Pokémon-style "super effective" feedback is a well-known pattern that turns hidden information into a learnable skill. The Pokémon chart (18×18) is far too big, though; 3–4 defenses is closer to 4e's scale.
- Avoid a dominant defense: if one defense (e.g., AC, as in 4e) is usually highest, attacks against it need a compensating bonus, or attacks against weaker defenses need a smaller rider. That is Schreiber's transitive balancing applied inside an intransitive structure.

## 5. Variance, swinginess, the "whiff factor" and player perception

### Takeaway
"Whiff factor" is an established RPG-theory term with Forge roots, defined as the effect of a high failure rate, especially when it does not match a character's expected competence. A flat d20 has about four times the variance of 3d6, so modifiers count for about twice as much under 3d6. Moving to a bell curve shifts outcomes toward stats but **does not remove misses** at middling odds. With a 60–65% hit chance, a hero has better than an 80% chance of at least one whiff in a four-turn fight. Players also misjudge probability: Sid Meier found players expect to win 3:1 fights essentially every time, and XCOM shipped a hidden "bad streak breaker" on easier difficulties. The direct anti-whiff tools in d20-family games are miss damage (13th Age, 4e, 5e 2024 Graze), a rising to-hit bonus (13th Age's escalation die), degrees of success (PF2), and banked excess success (2d20 Momentum).

### Cited Findings
**Whiff factor**
- [Established, Forge-derived] Whiff factor: "the effect of a high failure-rate for a given Resolution mechanic, especially when the rate does not accord with the character's expected competence." It is usually treated as a design flaw, and described as "a form of deprotagonization and disruptive to immersion". It probably comes from baseball slang for a missed swing, and D&D's 5% automatic miss on every attack is cited as an entry point. — [Bankuei, "A Closer Look at Whiff Factor" (2012)](https://bankuei.wordpress.com/2012/03/05/a-closer-look-at-whiff-factor/); [RPG Theory Review, "Lesson: Whiff Factor" (2007)](http://rpgtheoryreview.blogspot.com/2007/03/lesson-whiff-factor.html); [RPG.net, "Please explain 'the Whiff factor'"](https://forum.rpg.net/threads/please-explain-the-whiff-factor.189403/)
- [Community] Common fixes: make sure something happens every time dice are rolled; give failures partial effects; Savage Worlds bonuses that mean most tasks fail only on a 1; Fate aspect invokes; opposed tests in Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay. Counter-views: a character who can never miss is unrealistic, and some argue whiff only becomes a problem below about 90% success. — [EN World, "Eliminating the whiff factor"](https://www.enworld.org/threads/eliminating-the-whiff-factor.681410/); [Professional Casual, WFRP review (2022)](https://professionalcasual.com/2022/09/01/warhammer-fantasy-roleplay-core-rulebook-review-part-1-overview/)
- [Community] Missing attacks "are extremely common in early levels of 5e", and players feel stuck with no way to improve their odds. — [Steam, Baldur's Gate 3 discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1086940/discussions/0/3808408747682548714/?ctp=4). One proposed narrative fix is to describe misses as the enemy blocking, dodging or deflecting rather than the hero failing. — [r/DnD mirror, "Stop saying players miss" (Jul 2024)](https://lr.eu.psf.lt/r/DnD/comments/1e1uu1l/stop_saying_players_miss)

**Bell curves vs the flat d20**
- [Computed] Exact enumeration: 1d20 mean 10.5, variance 33.25, SD 5.77. 2d10 mean 11, variance 16.5, SD 4.06. 3d6 mean 10.5, variance 8.75, SD 2.96. P(≥8): d20 65%, 2d10 79%, 3d6 84%. P(≥11): d20 50%, 2d10 55%, 3d6 50%. P(≥14): d20 35%, 2d10 28%, 3d6 16%. P(≥17): d20 20%, 2d10 10%, 3d6 1.9%. So a bell curve helps the favored side and hurts the underdog. At a 50% target it changes nothing.
- [Computed] Whiff streaks under a flat binary roll. At 60% to hit: 40% miss per turn, 16% to miss twice in a row, 6.4% to land nothing in three turns, 87% to whiff at least once in four turns. At 65%: 82% to whiff at least once in four turns. Example: a level-1 hero at +5 vs AC 13 needs 8+ on d20, so 65% hit. A "graze if you miss by 4 or less" band (rolls 4–7) would add 20%, leaving 15% (rolls 1–3) where nothing happens.
- [Community, consistent across sources] 3d6 has about a quarter of the d20's variance, so its spread is about half as wide (1d10+5 plays similarly). Each +1 is worth about twice as much: about 7 percentage points near the middle vs a flat 5 on a d20. D&D's DC assumptions rest on the flat 5% steps, so a swap means rebalancing the whole system. — [EN World, "Replacing 1d20 with 3d6 is nearly pointless"](https://www.enworld.org/threads/replacing-1d20-with-3d6-is-nearly-pointless.669549/page-11); [RPG.net, "3d6 vs d20"](https://forum.rpg.net/index.php?threads%2F3d6-vs-d20.757685%2F=); [John H. Kim, "Dice Mechanics: Methods"](https://www.darkshire.net/jhkim/rpg/systemdesign/dice-methods.html); [Giant in the Playground, "d20 to 3d6 Gaming"](https://forums.giantitp.com/archive/index.php/t-72458.html)
- [Rules (secondary)] The "Bell Curve Rolls" variant (roll 3d6 wherever you would roll d20) comes from an **Unearthed Arcana** variant, mirrored on the d20 SRD variant pages. It is not in the 5e DMG: the search found no such DMG rule. It makes "a grittier d20 game" with "far fewer very good or very bad rolls". Rolls of 1, 2, 19 and 20 become impossible, and it "favors the stronger side in combat", which matters because monsters often depend on lucky hits. — [SRD (dndtools) "Rolls and Modifiers" variant](https://srd.dndtools.org/srd/variant/unearthedRolls.html); [D&D Wiki, "UA:Bell Curve Rolls"](https://www.dandwiki.com/wiki/UA:Bell_Curve_Rolls)

**Anti-whiff mechanics in d20-family games**
- [Rules (secondary)] 13th Age: many monster attacks carry "Miss: Half damage." The **escalation die** starts at 1 at the end of round one and goes up by one each round, adding to PC attack rolls, so misses get rarer as a fight goes on. — [Rob Heinsoo, "13th Age: The Two-Hour Demo" (PDF, Pelgrane 2012)](https://pelgranepress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/13th-Age-2-Hour-Demo.pdf). A designer-side post says "most attacks do some damage on a miss". — [Walking Mind (Evil Hat), "Combat in 13th Age" (2013)](https://walkingmind.evilhat.com/2013/08/30/combat-in-13th-age/)
- [Designer opinion] Heinsoo on giving the escalation die to monsters: doing so on all monster attacks would make them far deadlier and "would eliminate our game's dramatic curve". A rising PC-only bonus is meant to create a dramatic curve. — [Pelgrane, "13th Sage: Escalation for Everyone?"](https://site.pelgranepress.com/index.php/13th-sage-escalation-for-everyone/); see also [Pelgrane, "13th Sage: Escalate the Drama!" (2019)](https://pelgranepress.com/2019/02/01/13th-sage-escalate-the-drama/)
- [Rules (secondary)] D&D 2024 PHB, Graze mastery: if you miss a creature with the weapon, you deal damage equal to the ability modifier used for the attack roll. Per D&D Beyond's guide, no other limit applies. — [D&D Beyond, "Your Guide to Weapon Mastery in the 2024 Player's Handbook"](https://www.dndbeyond.com/posts/1742-your-guide-to-weapon-mastery-in-the-2024-players)
- [Designer opinion, reported] Weapon mastery first appeared in UA 2023 Player's Handbook Playtest 5. Jeremy Crawford framed it as addressing how underdeveloped weapon-focused martial classes feel, making weapons stand out and adding tactical decisions. Graze, "some damage even on a miss", was one example. — [TechRaptor, UA weapon mastery news](https://techraptor.net/tabletop/news/newest-unearthed-arcana-for-dd-features-weapon-mastery-rules); [Tribality, "UA 2023: Player's Handbook Playtest 5 Breakdown"](https://www.tribality.com/2023/04/28/ua-2023-players-handbook-playtest-5-breakdown/). [Community] Playtest feedback called Graze "totally overpowered" (unlimited, on any miss). A fan fix limits it to misses within your proficiency bonus. — [D&D Beyond forums, "Weapon Masteries revision"](https://www.dndbeyond.com/forums/dungeons-dragons-discussion/unearthed-arcana/177709-weapon-masteries-revision)
- [Rules (secondary)] Pathfinder 2e degrees of success: beat the DC by 10+ for a critical success, miss it by 10+ for a critical failure. A natural 20 or 1 shifts the result one degree. Paizo's 2018 playtest blog presented this as unifying earlier one-off margin rules. — [Roll20 PF2 compendium, "Checks"](https://roll20.net/compendium/pf2/Checks); [Paizo blog, "Critical Hits and Critical Failures"](https://paizo.com/blog/critical-hits-and-critical-failures)
- [Rules (secondary)] 2d20 Momentum: excess successes become a spendable, bankable pool (see section 1). — [Modiphius, "2d20 Basics"](https://modiphius.net/blogs/news/2d20-basics)

**Player perception of probability**
- [Designer opinion] Sid Meier's GDC 2010 keynote, "The Psychology of Game Design (Everything You Know Is Wrong)": a Civilization player who lost at displayed 3-to-1 odds felt cheated because "3 is such a bigger number than 1". Playtesters expect to win favorable battles even when probability says they sometimes will not. Meier argued that player psychology sometimes has to take priority over simulation accuracy, and the team adjusted odds to match players' sense of fairness. — [The Escapist, liveblog of Meier's GDC 2010 keynote](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/liveblog-sid-meiers-gdc-2010-keynote-speech/); [Strong Museum of Play, "GDC 2010: Game Psychology 101"](https://www.museumofplay.org/blog/gdc-2010-game-psychology-101/); [Ada Chen Rekhi, GDC10 notes](https://www.adachen.com/gdc10-notes-sid-meier-on-why-everything-you-know-is-wrong/)
- [Designer opinion, reported] At the same GDC, Rob Pardo (Blizzard, not Meier) described raising a quest item's drop rate after each failed kill until it reached 100%, then resetting it. That is a "pity timer". Coverage sometimes blends the two talks. — [Shacknews, "Sid Meier and Rob Pardo on Probability and Player Psychology"](https://www.shacknews.com/article/62807/sid-meier-and-rob-pardo)
- [Designer opinion, reported] XCOM: Enemy Within lead designer Ananda Gupta confirmed a hidden "luck" modifier that can override the displayed hit chance: "On Normal and Easy modes, there's a bad streak breaker." — [GameBanshee, "XCOM: Enemy Within Adds 'Save Scum' Option"](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/112504-xcom-enemy-within-adds-qsave-scumq-option.html). XCOM lead Jake Solomon on perception: "If you see an 85 percent chance to hit, you're not looking at that as a 15 percent chance of missing." — [CogConnected, "5 Ways XCOM 2 Warps Your Sense of Probability"](https://cogconnected.com/feature/xcom-2-warps-sense-probability/). Headline only, not read: [Softpedia, "Lead XCOM Designer Regrets Fixed Hit Chance Results"](https://games.softpedia.com/blog/Lead-XCOM-Designer-Regrets-Fixed-Hit-Chance-Results-319573.shtml)

### Inferences
- A bell curve (direction not proposed) would cut whiffs for favored heroes but make underdog attacks almost hopeless. At ≥14 needed, 3d6 gives 16% against the d20's 35%. That is a poor trade for an 8+ audience fighting tougher foes. Partial hits attack the whiff directly.
- A "miss by ≤ N becomes a graze" band works as a **variance-reduction tool that leaves the d20 intact**. At +5 vs AC 13 with N=4, "nothing happens" drops from 35% to 15% per attack. Fixed graze damage, like the 2024 Graze or 13th Age's half damage on a miss, needs no extra narration in play-by-post.
- A rising to-hit or momentum bonus per round or per miss (13th Age escalation die, Pardo's pity timer, XCOM's streak breaker) answers the **streak** problem, which is what players remember, as Meier and Solomon both describe. It fits a feature-flag "kid mode / easy mode" like XCOM's difficulty-gated streak breaker. Heinsoo's caution is to give it to heroes only, or monsters become too lethal.
- Do not show raw percentages as the main feedback for young players (see section 6). Visible tokens such as momentum pips are easier to read.

### Gaps
- I could not find primary writing on the whiff factor by The Angry GM, Sly Flourish or The Alexandrian specifically. Only the "In Defense of the Whiff Factor" title turned up, with no confirmed author.
- I found no official Crawford or WotC statement specifically defending Graze's miss damage or its balance, and no designer notes on a "Graze" option in 5e Unearthed Arcana beyond the 2023 weapon mastery playtest.
- The exact book and year of the "Bell Curve Rolls" variant: recollection is the 3.5e-era *Unearthed Arcana* hardcover (2004). **UNVERIFIED (memory).**
- I found no data on how often 5e tables actually see whiff turns. The streak figures above are pure arithmetic.

## 6. Onboarding and accessibility: combat that works for new and experienced players, including children

### Takeaway
The guidance is mostly practitioner wisdom rather than research. Key ideas: Bushnell's Law ("easy to learn, difficult to master"); Jakob Nielsen's progressive disclosure (1995), which defers advanced options until they are needed; Dan Cook's skill chains or skill atoms, where mechanics are taught in a dependency order; and introducing concepts gradually, as StarCraft II's campaign does. For children around 8, informal guides say they are just starting to handle more complex rules and strategy. Developmental research (Piaget & Inhelder and later replications) suggests formal probability reasoning develops later, while younger children's intuitions about chance are partial. That argues for concrete, visible outcomes over percentages, and for ensuring every turn does something.

### Cited Findings
- [Established] Bushnell's Law ("easy to learn, difficult to master") is credited to Atari founder Nolan Bushnell. It grew out of the confusing four-button controls of *Computer Space* (1971). Pong's instructions were reportedly one line. George Parker (Parker Brothers) expressed a similar idea about a century earlier. StarCraft II's campaign starts with marines only and adds units mission by mission, which is close to progressive disclosure. — [TL.net, "Game Study 1: Bushnell's Law"](https://tl.net/blogs/155657-game-study-1-bushnells-law)
- [Designer opinion, criticism] Ian Bogost argues Bushnell's Law should be retired, moving from "addiction" to "catchiness" in how video games are framed. — [Game Developer, "Persuasive Games: Familiarity, Habituation, and Catchiness"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/persuasive-games-familiarity-habituation-and-catchiness)
- [Established, UX] Progressive disclosure was introduced by Jakob Nielsen in 1995. It defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen, making software easier to learn and less error-prone; the macOS print dialog's "Show Details" is the standard example. — [MadUX, "Progressive disclosure"](https://madux.substack.com/p/progressive-disclosure); [UXPin, "What Is Progressive Disclosure in UX?"](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/?p=41489). The original Nielsen Norman Group article is nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/ (not opened).
- [Designer opinion + academic] Dan Cook (Lost Garden) models games as skill chains, "directed graphs of skill atoms or core loops that logically build on each other", which mirror learning hierarchies. Horn, Cooper & Deterding (CHI PLAY 2017) note that designers mostly build skill chains "based solely on design intent, theory, or personal observation" and propose deriving them empirically. Raph Koster argues the skills are nested rather than linear chains. — [Horn, Cooper & Deterding (NSF PAR PDF)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10049718); [Raph Koster, "Cooking up Chemistry"](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=1298). Cook's related idea of "loops and arcs" separates repeated interaction loops from progression arcs. — [UX Podcast, "Loops, arcs and terrain"](https://uxpodcast.com/loops-arcs-terrain/)
- [Community] Children: "by 6-8, children are beginning to learn somewhat more complicated rules and starting to understand the concept of strategy." — [Rempton Games, "Game Design for All Ages" (2017)](https://remptongames.com/2017/06/08/game-design-for-all-ages/). Hero Kids modules run 30–60 minutes to suit children's attention spans. — [d20 Collective, "6 Good Starter Tabletop RPGs for Kids"](https://d20collective.com/blogs/divinations-from-the-collective/6-good-starter-tabletop-rpgs-for-kids). Parents running RPGs for 7–8-year-olds advise starting with a simplified ruleset and adding rules as players show they are ready. — [Hacker News, "Ask HN: Tabletop RPGs for 7-8 Year-Olds"](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=17311663). Beginner-friendly games introduce concepts gradually over the first few turns to prevent overload. — [Board Games Guide, beginner recommendations](https://boardgamesguide.com/board-games-recommendations-beginners/)
- [Community] Young children dislike Chutes and Ladders because "there are zero choices to be made". — [Ideonexus, "Ten Mechanics to Make Candyland Bearable or Even Awesome" (2018)](https://ideonexus.com/2018/08/13/ten-mechanics-to-make-candyland-bearable-or-even-awesome/). The excerpt said a forum user found Candy Land's printed optional "draw two, choose one" rule teaches weighing options, but the page could not be pinned down; possibly [Heroscapers, "Candyland vs. Chutes and Ladders"](https://www.heroscapers.com/threads/candyland-vs-chutes-and-ladders.8315/).
- [Academic] Piaget & Inhelder studied how children tell chance events from deterministic ones and reason about random draws. A common summary places formal probability reasoning at about age 12. — [CAUSEweb summary](https://causeweb.org/cause/node/5908). Later work argues young children's probability intuitions are more structured than Piaget suggested. — [UCL Discovery paper (PDF)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1329372/2/1329372.pdf). Green & Ross (1978) tested 56 children aged 5–17: support for Piaget's verbal stage descriptions, not his nonverbal ones. — [CAUSEweb, replication of Piaget & Inhelder](https://www.causeweb.org/cause/node/5527). A probability-learning study grouping ages 5–6, 8–9 and 11–12 found that performance tracked developmental level. — [CAUSEweb research listing p.72](https://www.causeweb.org/cause/literature-category/research?page=72)
- [Designer opinion] Meier's GDC 2010 point that players perceive odds as fairer than they are (section 5) applies to all audiences. XCOM's streak breaker is limited to the easy difficulties, a precedent for mercy rules gated by difficulty. — [Escapist liveblog](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/liveblog-sid-meiers-gdc-2010-keynote-speech/); [GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/112504-xcom-enemy-within-adds-qsave-scumq-option.html)

### Inferences
- Progressive disclosure fits the feature-flag plan. Layer 1 (everyone): every attack does something, using a hit / graze / miss band with fixed numbers. Layer 2 (unlocked or opt-in): choosing which defense to target, with simple "weak spot" cues (a Pokémon-style "super effective!" message). Layer 3 (experienced or flagged): banking and spending momentum after the roll, which is fortune in the middle.
- For ages 8 and up, Costikyan's "no decisions = not a game" point, the Chutes-and-Ladders anecdote and the developmental findings point the same way. Give one clear, visible choice per turn, and show outcomes as concrete effects (damage, a momentum pip) rather than percentages.
- In play-by-post, a whiff costs more because a turn may take hours of real time. That makes "something happens on every roll" more valuable there than at a live table. This is an inference; I found no source on async play specifically.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed research on rules complexity or combat design for 8–12-year-olds in RPGs. The child-specific guidance is anecdotal.
- I could not open the Nielsen Norman Group original or Dan Cook's own Lost Garden essays ("The Chemistry of Game Design"). Their content here is secondhand.
- I found no designer guidance on onboarding in play-by-post or Discord RPGs specifically.
