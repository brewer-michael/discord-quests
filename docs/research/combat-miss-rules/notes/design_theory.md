## 1. Input vs output randomness, and "fortune in the middle" vs "fortune at the end"

### Takeaway
Two vocabularies say the same thing. Engelstein's own GDC 2018 slides put **input randomness** (random → decision → result) on the "strategic, high level of control, skillful" side and **output randomness** (decision → random → result) on the "tactical, feel random, lower skill" side. He also flags wargames as the exception: there, the skill lies in setting up good odds before the roll. Ron Edwards's Forge definitions match: in **fortune at the end** (FatE) everything is fixed before the roll and the dice have the "final word"; in **fortune in the middle** (FitM) the roll comes before the specifics are described and is then "employed in establishing these elements retroactively". He says he first articulated it in his Hero Wars review (2000), and his clearest wording is in an August 2001 Forge thread, including the line that under FitM "a miss is not a specific whiffed attempt". A d20 hit/miss with nothing to do afterward is plain output randomness / FatE. One small empirical study (18 players) cuts against the designer consensus: input randomness, not output, lowered satisfaction.

### Cited Findings
**Input vs output randomness**
- [Designer opinion, primary] [opened] Engelstein, "White, Brown, and Pink: The Flavors of Tabletop Game Randomness", GDC 2018 slides. They open by quoting Costikyan ("In designing games, a degree of uncertainty is essential"). The slides diagram input randomness as Random → Decision → Fixed result and output randomness as Fixed → Decision → Random result. The comparison slide reads: input "Strategic / High level of control / Skillful"; output "Tactical / Feel random / Lower skill", followed by "But... Wargames?". The second axis is **correlation**: white noise ("You know nothing", "the most common type of randomness in games"); brown noise ("You know a lot", a random walk); pink noise ("You have a pretty good idea, but could be wrong": a big chance of a small change and falling chances of larger ones), with the slide "Humans Like Pink Noise". Ways to generate pink noise: dice delta, exploding dice, card distribution, multiple dice, extending the extremes. Exploding-dice example: "1-3: Miss / 4-5: 1 Hit / 6: 2 Hits and Reroll". Closing design tips: "Consider Input versus Output Randomness / Planning, strategy, analysis paralysis", "People Like Pink Noise", "Try to be between totally random and totally certain". — [GDC 2018 slides (PDF)](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/Engelstein_Geoffrey_White_Brown_Pink.pdf), text extracted with pdftotext.
- [Community] [opened] Skeleton Code Machine defines input randomness as "a random event that happens before the player makes decisions" and output randomness as one where "the random event happens after the player has made their decision". Its example is Risk combat, and it says dice-based TTRPG checks are usually output randomness. Citing Engelstein's GameTek Classic 183 episode, it notes output randomness is "a hallmark of hex and counter" wargames, where the strategy is positioning so the odds favor you before combat. (No Carcassonne or poker examples here; the first pass mixed those in from other pages.) — [Skeleton Code Machine, "Input-Output Randomness (Part 1)", 28 Nov 2023](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/input-output-randomness-part-1)
- [Research] [opened] Zhang, Monteiro, Liang, Ma & Baghaei, "Effect of Input-output Randomness on Gameplay Satisfaction in Collectable Card Games" (arXiv 2107.08437). The literature review reports Engelstein's view that input randomness supports strategy while output randomness undercuts planning (citing his *Ludology* GameTek Classic 183, 2018), and quotes Burgun that "input randomness is definitely better than output randomness". It also cites Mark Brown (Game Maker's Toolkit, "Two Types of Random", 2020): carefully tuned output randomness can improve a game, while poorly designed input randomness can damage it. **Result:** 18 participants, four conditions, Latin-square order. Output randomness had **no significant effect** on satisfaction; the input-only condition had the **lowest** satisfaction (significant against no-randomness and output-only, p < 0.05). The authors say the study may be underpowered. — [arXiv 2107.08437 (PDF)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08437)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Isaac Shalev (Engelstein's co-author) summarizes Keith Burgun: "Burgun places randomness on a spectrum between these poles", and "The less time a player has to respond to the random event, the more Burgun would describe the random event as output random". Rolling a die for combat is "clearly output randomness". — [Kind Fortress, "Design Patterns: Random Loops", 3 Oct 2018](https://www.kindfortress.com/2018/10/03/design-patterns-random-loops/)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Burgun says randomness matters but "not all randomness is equal", and that extremely random multiplayer strategy games can seem to work because players cannot tell luck from their own strategy. — [Keith Burgun, "Why You Need the Clockwork Game", 30 Dec 2015](http://keithburgun.net/why-you-need-the-clockwork-game/)
- [opened] Engelstein & Shalev, *Building Blocks of Tabletop Game Design*, 2nd ed., CRC Press 2022, 626 pp. Chapters include "4 Resolution" and "6 Uncertainty"; each entry gives a definition, a diagram, example games and design considerations. The publisher page shows no entry list, and I could not read the chapter text. — [Routledge, 2nd edition page](https://www.routledge.com/Building-Blocks-of-Tabletop-Game-Design-An-Encyclopedia-of-Mechanisms/Engelstein-Shalev/p/book/9781032015811)

**Fortune in the middle (FitM) vs fortune at the end (FatE)**
- [Designer opinion, primary] [opened] Forge thread "What is Fortune in the Middle" (started 9 Aug 2001). Ron Edwards, reply #1: "I think I articulated it first in my review of Hero Wars". FatE means "all player and GM contribution to a character's action is established prior to rolling … and the 'final word' on the success of the action is given by the Fortune outcome." In *Hero Wars* the player announces "ONLY general intent prior to rolling … Not, 'I shoot my arrow at him,' but, 'I put him out of action with my arrow shot.'" After the roll, "now is the time to take into consideration the Bump mechanic and whether you'll spend a Hero Point to bump." On failing: "Much more dignified; a miss is not a specific whiffed attempt." In the same post he calls FitM "the main reason why Narrativist RPG design may still make excellent use of Fortune mechanics at all", and lists Castle Falkenstein's magic among his examples. (The first pass credited that line to GNS Chapter 4; it is in this thread.) Reply #4: if a combat system is abstract enough (AD&D c. 1980), "you're all set with Fortune-in-the-Middle if you want to use it", but in practice players "ignore ANY interpretation … and merely follow the numbers". From RuneQuest on, "Fortune-at-the-End rules the roost". — [Forge archive, topic 442](http://indie-rpgs.com/archive/index.php?topic=442.0)
- [Designer opinion, primary] [opened] Edwards, *Hero Wars* review (dated 2000-11-01). It describes the mechanics he later named, without using the term: bumping ("shifting one's success upward by a category, either by expending Hero Points … or by having a high enough Mastery"), augmentation (success on one roll gives a bonus on the next), and Extended Contests, where Action Point losses are interpreted "in retrospect" once one side drops below zero. Edwards's one worry is handling time. — [The Forge, Reviews: Hero Wars](http://www.indie-rpgs.com/reviews/16/)
- [Designer opinion, primary] [opened] Forge glossary. FitM: "Employing a Fortune Resolution technique … prior to fully describing the specific actions … The Fortune outcome is employed in establishing these elements retroactively", either with the dice as final authority (Sorcerer) or with the outcome "potentially adjusted by a metagame mechanic (e.g. HeroQuest)". FatE: "following the full descriptions of actions". — [The Forge, Provisional Glossary](http://www.indie-rpgs.com/_articles/glossary.html)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] GNS Chapter 4 (Edwards, 14 Oct 2001) defines Drama, Karma and Fortune resolution and points to "My on-line discussion of Fortune-in-the-Middle as a facilitator of Narrativist play". — [The Forge, GNS Chapter 4](https://www.indie-rpgs.com/articles/5/)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Justin Alexander: Edwards "coined the terms fortune in the middle and fortune at the end". FatE is "Establish method. Check the fortune. Describe the result." and "seems to be what most GMs and groups default to". For a single attack, fortune at the beginning "generally isn't useful". He gives no FitM example for an attack roll. — [The Alexandrian, "Art of Rulings 10: Fortune Positioning", 17 May 2016](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/38570/roleplaying-games/art-of-rulings-10-fortune-positioning)
- [Rules (secondary)] [opened] Modiphius 2d20: "If you get more successes than the GM asked for, you gain something called Momentum!", spent to improve a success (more information, extra actions, restoring Spirit); the GM spends Threat. [search extract] The pool cap of 6 in *Conan* and spends such as extra damage or +1d20 come only from excerpts. — [Modiphius, "2d20 Basics", 20 Nov 2023](https://modiphius.net/blogs/news/2d20-basics); [Black Gate, Conan 2d20 report](https://www.blackgate.com/?p=445845) (not opened)

### Inferences
- Today's d20 hit/miss is output randomness / FatE, so whiff turns follow from the structure, not from bad luck.
- Direction 2 (stats pick the targeted defense) is Engelstein's wargame case: the skill is in choosing good odds *before* the roll. It makes the decision more meaningful but does not remove empty turns.
- Direction 1 (partial hits plus momentum) brings in FitM traits. A banked momentum that changes the *next* roll also changes the noise from white toward correlated ("pink"): a bad turn makes the next one likelier to land. Engelstein's "humans like pink noise" slide supports that direction, though he does not apply it to combat.
- The Zhang et al. result is a reason not to over-trust the "output randomness is bad" rule of thumb. It is one small card-game study, though, and says nothing about streaks.
- Edwards's "a miss is not a specific whiffed attempt" is a presentation rule the bot can apply at no cost: narrate a miss as the foe's defense working, not as the hero failing (see section 5, Angry GM).
- Other FitM patterns, all **UNVERIFIED (memory)**: 5e Bardic Inspiration and Lucky (used after seeing the d20), Battle Master Precision Attack, Blades in the Dark resistance rolls, Roll for the Galaxy dice assignment. Check them against the SRD or rulebooks before citing.

### Gaps
- *Building Blocks* and *GameTek* (HarperCollins 2020) text not seen. The Ludology episodes (GameTek Classic 183; Episode 34) were not heard.
- BoardGameGeek was blocked, so the claim that Lewis Pulsipher credits Engelstein with coining "output randomness" is still **[search extract]**. The slides show Engelstein using the terms; they do not prove he coined them.
- The Drama/Karma/Fortune trio's origin in *Everway* (Tweet, 1995) is still **UNVERIFIED (memory)**. GNS Chapter 4 uses the terms but does not credit them.

## 2. Greg Costikyan, *Uncertainty in Games* (MIT Press, 2013)

### Takeaway
Costikyan names **eleven** sources of uncertainty, and randomness is only one of them. His 1994 essay defines a game by decisions ("The thing that makes a game a game is the need to make decisions"). He calls Candy Land an exception that "inolves [sic] no decision making whatsoever", and argues that over many rolls the dice average out, so the better strategist usually wins. Taken together: a turn that resolves into nothing the player can act on is a failure of decision-making, not of uncertainty as such.

### Cited Findings
- [opened] Bibliographic facts: MIT Press, second volume in the Playful Thinking series (series editors Jesper Juul, Geoffrey Long, William Uricchio), 2013. Thesis: games need uncertainty to hold players' interest, and mastering uncertainty is central to their appeal. Examples range from Super Mario Bros. and Rock/Paper/Scissors to Monopoly, CityVille, FPS deathmatch and Chess. — [Jesper Juul, The Ludologist, 30 May 2013](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2013/05/30/greg-costikyan-uncertainty-in-games/). [search extract] Paperback 2015, ISBN 9780262527538 (the MIT Press page returned 403).
- [Established] [opened] The eleven sources, as Evan Torner lists them: "performative uncertainty," "solver's uncertainty," "player unpredictability," "randomness," "analytic complexity," "hidden information," "narrative anticipation," "development anticipation," "schedule uncertainty," "uncertainty of perception," and "semiotic uncertainty." — [Analog Game Studies, Torner, "Uncertainty in Analog Role-Playing Games, Part 2" (Sept 2014)](https://analoggamestudies.org/2014/09/uncertainty-in-analog-role-playing-games-part-2/)
- [Research] [opened] Power et al. also say Costikyan "suggests 11 sources of uncertainty" and define each one, e.g. randomness "refers to uncertainty emanate from random game elements". For their questionnaire they dropped three (schedule, development anticipation, semiotic) "as these were deemed to happen to a player outside of the act of playing itself". That likely explains the smaller counts in some later papers. — [Power et al., "Measuring Uncertainty in Games: Design and Preliminary Validation", CHI 2017 LBW (PDF)](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/21355/1/lbw0607-powerA.pdf)
- [Designer opinion via reviewer] [opened] Liz England lists ten of the sources (all but semiotic) with examples, and quotes the book that "a single throw of dice is extremely dependent on randomness", while many throws tend "to temper that randomness with strategic choices". — [Liz England, review (4 Oct 2016)](https://lizengland.com/blog/review-uncertainty-in-games-by-greg-costikyan/)
- [Community] [opened] The Thoughtful Gamer: "randomness is merely one of the many types of uncertainty Costikyan talks about". Uncertainty about what another person will do "is arguably more interesting". The reviewer calls the book's "100 or so pages" densely packed. (The first pass's "a perhaps too brief look" quote is not on this page; removed.) — [The Thoughtful Gamer, 19 Jul 2018](https://thethoughtfulgamer.com/2018/07/19/uncertainty-in-games-book-review/)
- [Designer opinion, primary] [opened] Costikyan, "I Have No Words & I Must Design" (Interactive Fantasy #2, 1994). Definition: "A game is a form of art in which participants, termed players, make decisions in order to manage resources through game tokens in the pursuit of a goal." Also: "Interaction must have purpose" and "The thing that makes a game a game is the need to make decisions." Candy Land is named as the exception that "inolves [sic] no decision making whatsoever". On dice: "'Random elements' in a game are never wholly random", and "Except in rare cases, my victory or defeat will be based on my excellence as a strategist, not on my luck with the dice." — [Mirror of the 1994 text at bearstrong.net](https://bearstrong.net/tekst97/data/programmering/gamesprog/nowords/) (costik.com original returned 404)

### Inferences
- Costikyan supports keeping real dice and adding uncertainty that does not cost a turn. Hidden information (which defense is weak?) and analytic complexity (which move against which foe?) fit direction 2.
- His "many rolls dilute luck" argument does not cover a short Discord fight: with 3–5 attacks per hero, one or two misses decide how the fight felt. That favors streak-breaking (momentum) over relying on averages.
- For 8+, the 1994 definition backs giving at least one real decision per turn.

### Gaps
- The book's own text on randomness versus agency was not seen (MIT Press blocked, no preview found). My recollection that he argues randomness helps weaker players and eases analysis paralysis is still **UNVERIFIED (memory)**.
- The first pass's claim that Costikyan says Candy Land and Chutes and Ladders "are not games" came from a secondhand blog. The 1994 essay treats Candy Land as an *exception to his theory*, not as a non-game, and does not mention Chutes and Ladders. Removed.
- Torner's point that transparency gives players more agency and more responsibility: the ETC Press PDF did not load, so it is unconfirmed and removed from findings.

## 3. "Fail forward" / "failing forward" in RPG design

### Takeaway
"Fail forward" means a failed roll still moves play forward, usually as success with a cost or as a new problem. **No one is credited with coining it.** An RPG.net search of the term's history found its earliest online use in 2008, wider use from 2010, and 13th Age as the first game to print it as a named rule. 13th Age's version covers only checks **outside** battle. The criticisms are well sourced: the name is business jargon (Run a Game; OSR critics); PbtA writers say it misdescribes moves; partial results add GM adjudication work (Drolleries); and a weak hit that is "just a GM move" is "effectively a miss" (Sage LaTorra).

### Cited Findings
- [Community] [opened] RPG.net, "The origins of fail forward" (Sept 2017). Bankuei: "The first formal discussions of 'Fail Forward' I saw came out of the Forge Forums, in the early 2000s." Later_Man guesses Robin Laws, without evidence. Others name Dogs in the Vineyard (2004), Burning Wheel Classic (2002), Sorcerer & Sword (2001), MERP's static maneuver table, and wandering monsters as precursors. CarpeGuitarrem calls 13th Age "the one RPG that I recall explicitly called it 'fail forward'". User Incharge's term search found the earliest online use in a 2008 forum post, wider use from 2010, Ryan Macklin (2010) crediting InSpectres (2002) for "fail-forward concepts", first RPG.net use about 2013, and one Forge use in 2012. — [RPG.net thread](https://forum.rpg.net/index.php?threads/the-origins-of-fail-forward.815557/)
- [Rules, primary] [opened] 13th Age SRD, "Fail Forward": "Outside of battle, when failure would tend to slow action down rather than move the action along", the GM should "instead interpret it as a near-success or event that happens to carry unwanted consequences or side effects." Natural 1: "the skill check fumbles and fails, perhaps in a particularly bad way." License: Open Game License (13th Age Archmage Engine 3.0, © 2013–2015 Fire Opal Media). — [13th Age SRD, "Running the Game"](https://www.13thagesrd.com/running-the-game/)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Justin Alexander: failing forward means the failure is "described as being a success-with-complications in the game world". It is the second of three intro techniques after "No Retries", with the default "You succeed, but…" plus a cost. He warns that players grow frustrated if they never accomplish anything. — [The Alexandrian, "Failure for the Beginning GM", 10 Apr 2021](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/46186/roleplaying-games/failure-for-the-beginning-gm)
- [Community] [opened] Run a Game: the term comes from business self-help and was adopted "because it sounds good", "a very bad reason to pick a term"; it proposes Fate Core's "succeed at a cost". — [Run a Game, "Fail Forward", 30 Dec 2015](https://www.runagame.net/2015/12/fail-forward.html)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Jason D'Angelo: "The dice don't decide success or failure; they decide how much you have to offer, risk, or endure to get what you seek." Calling a 6- "failing forward" shows how strongly players default to the success/failure binary. — [The Daily Apocalypse #143, 10 Dec 2018](https://daily-apocalypse.com/daily-apocalypse/143-beyond-success-failure-failing-forward-and-moves-in-apocalypse-world)
- [Rules + Designer opinion] [opened] Dungeon World Hack and Slash: "On a 7–9, you deal your damage to the enemy and the enemy makes an attack against you." Co-designer Sage LaTorra: "The reason the player gets something on a 7–9 is that it's a partial success," and "Making it just a GM move would make it effectively a miss." — [Dungeon World G+ archive, 10 Dec 2012](https://dungeonworld.gplusarchive.online/2012/12/10/the-hack-and-slash-move-says-on-a-79-you-deal-your-damage-to-the-enemy-and-the-enemy-makes-an-attack-against)
- [Community] [opened] Colin Spiridonov: mixed results make the GM invent a fitting complication every time. In FIST, partial successes came up about 45% of the time, and matching consequences in long combats was hard. Monster of the Week's moves say what a 7–9 means, and Daggerheart's Fear lets the GM bank a consequence. — [Drolleries, "The GM Burden of Adjudicating Partial Successes", 9 Jun 2025](https://drolleries.substack.com/p/the-gm-burden-of-adjudicating-partial)
- [Community] [opened] OSR criticism: players want success "with some lame 'consequence' attached" instead of accepting failure. It is a "nonsensical notion that every PC action should meaningfully advance progress", since a failed attempt already rules out one option. A defender concedes the name "confuses people into thinking forward always means good for players". — [OSRPG Talk, "The Old School: No 'fail forward'", 22 Sep 2025](https://osrpgtalk.net/the-old-school-no-fail-forward/)
- [Community] [opened] A d20 variant adapted from Blades in the Dark: on a failed attack, 20 minus the roll becomes the opponent's attack roll, so a miss can become an enemy hit. The goal is to make "the fight faster and failure more interesting". When both sides miss, the GM still has to explain why. Tested in one session only. — [Komodo Games, "Speeding up combat with failure states", Michael Dozark, 13 May 2025](https://komodogames.substack.com/p/speeding-up-combat-with-failure-states)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Evil Hat on GUMSHOE: "if a clue is there to be found, *it is found*", and failed general rolls offer the player nothing, so it proposes refunding a pool point on failure. — [Evil Hat, "The Whiff Factor: GUMSHOE Edition", 23 May 2008 (updated 2022)](https://evilhat.com/the-whiff-factor-gumshoe-edition/). [search extract] Pelgrane's warning that automatic clues can feel less earned: [Pelgrane, "Giving Out Clues in GUMSHOE" (2016)](https://pelgranepress.com/2016/03/15/giving-out-clues-in-gumshoe/) (not opened).
- [search extract] Two forum sources were not opened. A Paizo thread argues for designs "where the story advances no matter what the result". An EN World thread house-rules a fail-by-1–2 near miss as success with a complication. — [Paizo forums](https://paizo.com/threads/rzs7yvdh?I-wish-Paizo-would-embrace-Fail-Forward-game=); [EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/do-you-use-the-success-w-complication-module-in-the-dmg-or-fail-forward-in-the-basic-pdf.680103/)
- [Rules (secondary)] [search extract] PbtA 2d6 bands (6- miss, 7–9 partial, 10+ full). — [Designers & Dragons, "The Evolution of the Apocalypse"](https://www.designers-and-dragons.com/2021/11/02/the-evolution-of-the-apocalypse-a-system-history/)

### Inferences
- The Dungeon World 7–9 ("you hit, and something happens to you") is the closest model for a combat partial hit, and LaTorra's point sets a floor: a partial hit must give the hero something real, or it is a miss in disguise. For children the "cost" side can be dropped and replaced with momentum.
- The GM-burden criticism falls away for a bot if partial outcomes are **mechanically fixed** (set graze damage, a momentum token), not improvised narration. Drolleries' Monster of the Week and Daggerheart examples point the same way.
- 13th Age deliberately keeps fail forward out of battle. Combat miss rules need their own justification (miss damage, the escalation die; see `d20_systems.md`).

### Gaps
- The coiner is still unknown. I did not find Macklin's 2010 post.
- The 5e DMG "Success at a Cost" wording is still unconfirmed (task A).
- Burning Wheel's "let it ride" and "intent and task" principles were not sourced.
- Whether John C. Maxwell's *Failing Forward* dates to 2000 or 2007 is unresolved, and it does not matter to the design.

## 4. Meaningful choice and dominant strategies, applied to choosing which defense to target

### Takeaway
Meier's GDC 2012 tests are concrete. A decision is not interesting if players always pick the same option or if the result is random. Interesting decisions involve "some kind of tradeoff", are "situational", let players "express your personal play style", and are "persistent". He advises erring toward too much information rather than too little. Sirlin's balance test is "a reasonably large number of options … are viable", and "the worst thing" is a dominant move. Schreiber calls intransitive (rock-paper-scissors) structures an "emergency brake" on dominant strategies but warns they are "very fragile". D&D 4e's four defenses are the cautionary case: "all within spitting distance of each other" (Necropraxis). 5e 2024 masteries show the other failure, a fixed rider with no reason not to use it ("you will use them every single attack"). Darkest Dungeon's resistance tables are the working example.

### Cited Findings
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Meier (as reported by Leigh Alexander): "It's easier to look at it as what is not an interesting decision": always picking the same option, or an outcome chosen at random. Good decisions "involve some kind of tradeoff", are "situational", let you "express your personal play style", and "are persistent". On information, he would rather risk "providing the player with too much information". — [Game Developer, "GDC 2012: Sid Meier on how to see games as sets of interesting decisions", 7 Mar 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] The Escapist dates the line "A game is a series of interesting decisions" to GDC 1989, and quotes the 2012 talk: "something that's interesting you do once doesn't mean it's something interesting to do ten times." — [The Escapist, GDC 2012 report](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/gdc-2012-sid-meier-sees-interesting-decisions-even-in-rhythm-games/)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Sirlin: "A multiplayer game is balanced if a reasonably large number of options available to the player are viable", "especially, but not limited to, during high-level play by expert players" (Part 1, Dec 2001). Part 2 (15 Aug 2014): "The worst thing you can have in a competitive multiplayer game is a dominant move," and "moves need to have counters" (Yomi layers 0–3; layer 4 is rarely needed). When options look different, "it's a lot harder for players to judge the relative value of moves", a skill he calls valuation. — [Sirlin, Part 1](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-1-definitions); [Part 2](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-2-viable-options)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Game Balance Concepts, Ian Schreiber's 2010 online course. The blog carries no byline, but reader pingbacks call it "Ian Schreiber's New Summer School". Level 3: "intransitive is like Rock-Paper-Scissors, where everything is better than something else and there is no single 'best' move. In transitive games, some things are just flat out better than others … and we balance that by giving them different costs." Level 9: intransitive means "no single dominant strategy, because everything can be beaten by something else", "a kind of 'emergency brake' on runaway dominant strategies". Plain RPS is dull, and a slight bias can create a dominant counter, so the structure is "very fragile". "Tabletop RPGs often have some character classes that are particularly good at fighting against other classes." Uneven payoffs (doubling Rock's wins) change the solved mix to Paper 1/2, Rock 1/4, Scissors 1/4. — [Level 3, 21 Jul 2010](https://gamebalanceconcepts.wordpress.com/2010/07/21/level-3-transitive-mechanics-and-cost-curves/); [Level 9, 1 Sep 2010](https://gamebalanceconcepts.wordpress.com/2010/09/01/level-9-intransitive-mechanics/)

(The first pass's quote "so players always use them" is not in the thread; corrected.) — [D&D Beyond forums, "Weapon masteries lack turn-by-turn decision making", Oct 2023](https://www.dndbeyond.com/forums/dungeons-dragons-discussion/unearthed-arcana/182338-weapon-masteries-lack-turn-by-turn-decision-making)

- [Community] [search extract] 5e "target the weak save": Dex/Con/Wis are the strong saves and Str/Int/Cha the weak ones, and finding the weak save usually takes metagaming. A Paizo poster suggests a knowledge check that reveals the weakest defense. — [Paizo forums](https://paizo.com/threads/rzs42oxf&page=2); [EN World](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=6325554)

### Inferences
- Direction 2 passes Meier's test only if foes' defenses **differ enough to change the best choice**. 4e shows that within a point or two, the choice disappears. `sim/defenses.py` already finds AC the weakest defense for 84% of level-1 foes. If that holds, "always pick the weapon" becomes the dominant move, Meier's "always pick the same option".
- If a hero's stats alone pick the defense, the choice moves to character building. To keep a choice **each turn**, a hero needs two moves aimed at different defenses, and foes need visible weaknesses. Meier's "too much information" advice and the Paizo idea argue for **showing** the weak spot, not making players dig for it.
- Schreiber's fragility warning applies: a defense mix that is nearly balanced on paper will tip into a dominant pick if one defense is lower on average. Tune the constant (14 + save vs 12 + save) so each defense is the best target for a real share of the bestiary.
- A passive rider like Graze is not a decision (the masteries thread). If miss outcomes include a choice (bank momentum now, or spend it), that choice should be one a child can make in a second.
- For 8+, Pokémon's "super effective!" shows that type matchups are learnable at that age. Three defenses is closer to 4e's four than to an 18×18 chart.

### Gaps
- No 4e designer commentary on why four defenses were chosen (task A).
- No designer-authored analysis of Pokémon's type chart as intransitive design.
- Keith Burgun's definition of a game as a "contest of ambiguous decision-making" is still **UNVERIFIED (memory)**.
- Schreiber's "fruity" (unique, playtest-only) balancing is not on the course pages I opened (Levels 1, 2, 3, 6, 9). It is known only from secondary slides and notes, so it is removed from findings.

## 5. Variance, swinginess, the "whiff factor" and player perception

### Takeaway
The Forge glossary defines **whiff factor** as "the effect of a high failure-rate for a given Resolution mechanic, especially when the rate does not accord with the character's expected competence", "usually considered a Design flaw". No source names a coiner. The strongest counterpoint is Robin Laws (2012): a miss is not a null result ("Swinging and missing represents a loss for the player"), and consolation damage risks "merely putting a new coat of paint on a skewed ratio". Damage on a miss has been "popular with designers since 4e … and unpopular with users" (Tribality on the Graze playtest) and is "difficult to visualize and explain" (Rob Donoghue). WotC's printed rationale for masteries is thin: "giving you new ways to use weapons" (UA 2023) and "more choice in combat" (D&D Beyond 2024). I found no first-party rationale for Graze itself. The 3d6 variant is from *Unearthed Arcana* (2004, via the d20 SRD). Its own text says it "favors the stronger side in combat … almost always the PCs".

### Cited Findings
**Whiff factor**
- [Established, Forge] [opened] Forge glossary, "Whiff Factor": "The effect of a high failure-rate for a given Resolution mechanic, especially when the rate does not accord with the character's expected competence. A common source of Deprotagonizing; usually considered a Design flaw." — [The Forge, Provisional Glossary](http://www.indie-rpgs.com/_articles/glossary.html)
- [Community] [opened] Bankuei widens it: "Whiff factor is what you get when you have mechanics that do not change the situation", including "negligible change". His fixes: No Passive Results ("each resolution means one side or the other comes out ahead"), Defense Feeds Offense (a successful defense should "at least give a bonus to respond on the next action"), Degrading Defense, and Fictional Positioning Pushes. — [Deeper in the Game, "A Closer Look at Whiff Factor", 5 Mar 2012](https://bankuei.wordpress.com/2012/03/05/a-closer-look-at-whiff-factor/)
- [Community] [opened] Matthew George: "a higher-than-desired rate of failure in a mechanic used for conflict resolution, particularly physical combat"; when it clashes with expected competence "it is considered to be a form of deprotagonization and disruptive to immersion". He speculates the term "probably" comes from baseball and entered RPG culture through D&D, where "every attack has a random 5% chance of failure". — [RPG Theory Review, "Lesson: Whiff Factor", 23 Mar 2007](http://rpgtheoryreview.blogspot.com/2007/03/lesson-whiff-factor.html)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Robin D. Laws, "In Defense of the Whiff Factor" (15 Feb 2012). The null-result rule is "never ask for a roll if failure would lead to a dead end or other uninteresting result", but a combat miss is different: "Swinging and missing represents a loss for the player", "you've fallen behind, losing a chance to score". He warns that consolation prizes risk "merely putting a new coat of paint on a skewed ratio between the two". The real job is a satisfying ratio of success to failure. — [Laws, Goodreads author blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/2096338-in-defense-of-the-whiff-factor)
- [Community] [opened] EN World, "Eliminating the whiff factor" (16 Jul 2021): make "something" happen every roll, limited to basic attacks and cantrips. Supporter: "Every roll moves one side or the other towards its goal." Critics: it makes it "inadvisable to attack a hard hitting enemy unless you're fairly confident you can hit" and "makes multiple attacks worse". (The "below about 90%" counter-view from the first pass is not on page 1; removed.) — [EN World thread](https://www.enworld.org/threads/eliminating-the-whiff-factor.681410/)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] The Angry GM on narrating misses: "If the monster is nimble and lightly armored, describe misses as dodges"; armored foes see attacks "bouncing off its shell"; "Do not let your narration confuse what's happening in the game"; and "Hit points are hit points." — [The Angry GM, "Barking at Your Players: Advanced Combat Narration", 15 Mar 2019](https://theangrygm.com/barking-at-your-players-advanced-combat-narration/)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Justin Alexander on 4e minions: his players objected less to 1 hp than to the "never take damage on a miss" clause, because it showed them which foes were minions. — [The Alexandrian, "Playtesting 4th Edition – Part 1: Combat", 13 Jul 2008](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1941/roleplaying-games/playtesting-4th-edition-part-1-combat). **Damage on a miss and WotC's Graze**

### Inferences
- Laws's argument and the "unpopular with users" history mean flat miss damage is the hardest version to sell, and the sim agrees it "guarantees every miss pays". A **near-miss band** keeps real misses (Laws's loss), and **momentum** answers streaks, which is what Meier's players actually resent.
- Donoghue's "difficult to visualize" and the Angry GM's "do not let your narration confuse" point the same way: narrate a graze as a small, concrete thing (a nick, a stagger, the foe off balance) and say plainly that it counts.
- The minion lesson: outcomes that reveal hidden rules ("why did my graze do nothing to that one?") pull players out. If foes are immune to grazes, show it up front.
- The bell-curve variant favors PCs because monsters rely on lucky shots. That is a reason against it for a game with real dice and an XP-budget balancer: it would shift every encounter's odds at once.

- No first-party rationale for Graze specifically: the UA PDFs have none, Crawford's playtest video was not reachable as text, and TechRaptor was blocked.
- No data on how often real 5e tables see whiff turns.

## 6. Onboarding and accessibility: combat for new and experienced players, including children

### Takeaway
Nielsen's progressive disclosure is the right frame, with a constraint the first pass missed: designs with more than two levels "typically have low usability". Children's RPGs scale rules by age in exactly this way: *No Thank You, Evil!* has "different rule levels, each one geared toward a particular age group, from 5 on up" to about 10–11. They avoid hit points and arithmetic, and they turn failure into a resource: *Kids on Bikes* gives an Adversity Token on a failed roll, used to "bump up later rolls". The research cautions against removing failure altogether. Juul found the happiest players were those who "failed some, and then completed the game". Mueller & Dweck found that how failure is *framed* (effort versus ability) changed fifth graders' persistence, though a 2017 working paper failed to replicate the effect. Formal probability reasoning arrives around 12 (Piaget & Inhelder), which argues for concrete effects over percentages. WotC rates its own 2025 starter set 12+, so Discord Quests' 8+ is below the official D&D floor.

### Cited Findings
**Progressive disclosure and skill chains**
- [Established, UX] [opened] Nielsen: progressive disclosure "defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen", and improves "learnability, efficiency of use, and error rate". It is right only if "You must get the right split between initial and secondary features" and "It must be obvious how users progress from the primary to the secondary disclosure levels." Designs with more than two levels "typically have low usability because users often get lost when moving between the levels." He calls the idea "more than 30 years old" (the first pass's "introduced by Nielsen in 1995" is not supported by this page; removed). — [Nielsen Norman Group, "Progressive Disclosure", 3 Dec 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- [Research] [opened] Horn, Cooper & Deterding (CHI PLAY 2017): skill atoms "build and depend on each other in a nested learning hierarchy", and games should introduce them "in the ideal sequence". Designers "typically construct and use hypothetical skill chains based solely on design intent, theory, or personal observation", and Dan Cook's model treats games as "directed graphs of skill atoms". — [Horn, Cooper & Deterding (PDF)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10049718)
- [Designer opinion] [opened] Dan Cook's Lost Garden post introducing skill atoms and chains (19 Jul 2007). It links out to the full essay; in comments he stresses that games give feedback "on much tighter time scales" than school. — [Lost Garden, "The Chemistry of Game Design"](https://lostgarden.com/2007/07/19/the-chemistry-of-game-design/)
- [Established] [search extract] Bushnell's Law ("easy to learn, difficult to master"), and StarCraft II adding units mission by mission. TL.net returned 403. — [TL.net, "Game Study 1: Bushnell's Law"](https://tl.net/blogs/155657-game-study-1-bushnells-law)

**Children's RPGs and age guidance**
- [Designer, publisher] [opened] Monte Cook Games on *No Thank You, Evil!*: "fast, easy, and fun for kids as young as 5"; "there are different rule levels, each one geared toward a particular age group, from 5 on up", with the top level "probably around 10 or 11"; "families can play together easily, even if the players cover a wide age range"; tokens mean "very little writing or math". — [Monte Cook Games, No Thank You, Evil!](https://www.montecookgames.com/no-thank-you-evil/)
- [Community] [opened] Review of NTYE: tasks are 1–6 and "you have to roll the Goal number or higher"; "if you pay a token to the Guide you can lower the Goal number by one"; an Awesome token buys a reroll; "we didn't keep hit points". — [Meeple Mountain, Jonathan Elder, 12 Jan 2018](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/no-thank-you-evil-review-a-role-playing-game-for-kids/)
- [Community] [opened] *Kids on Bikes*: characters earn Adversity Tokens when they fail a roll, used to "bump up later rolls"; "Failure isn't all bad either". The bonus size is not stated, and the Roll20 rules pages are paywalled. (Kids on Bikes is a game *about* kids, not one designed for child players.) — [Nerdist, Rob Wieland, 14 Nov 2017](https://nerdist.com/article/explore-stranger-mysteries-and-other-things-as-kids-on-bikes/)
- [Community] [opened] *Hero Kids* (ages 4–10, sessions of 30–60 minutes): both sides roll d6 pools and "You only compare the highest number from each side." One reviewer rewarded the whole party, because individual competition caused arguments. Ties and miss handling are not described. — [Elvyler, 12 Jan 2021](https://elvyler.com/2021/01/hero-kids-ttrpg-review); [Engaged Family Gaming review](https://engagedfamilygaming.com/tabletop-rpg-review-hero-kids/)
- [Community] [opened] *Amazing Tales* (ages 4+): rules "fit on one page", and much of the book is "tips on how to handle success and failure" (not detailed). — [Nerdist, Raf Cordero, 20 Feb 2019](https://nerdist.com/article/what-rpgers-gms-can-learn-from-playing-amazing-tales-an-rpg-for-kids-aged-4/)
- [Publisher listing] [opened] D&D Starter Set: Heroes of the Borderlands: "Age 12+", "Designed for 3-5 players of any skill level", with a combat tracker that "teaches new Dungeon Masters how to track initiative". — [Asmodee Canada listing](https://shop.asmodee.ca/en/wtcd4705)
- [Community] [opened] Rempton Games: at 6–8, kids are "beginning to learn somewhat more complicated rules" and "starting to understand the concept of strategy", but "math (beyond basic addition and subtraction) can be difficult". At 9–12 they "begin to learn from their mistakes in order to improve at the game". — [Rempton Games, "Game Design for All Ages", 8 Jun 2017](https://remptongames.com/2017/06/08/game-design-for-all-ages/)
- [Community] [opened] Ask HN, RPGs for 7–8-year-olds (14 Jun 2018): "Take the system that you are most comfortable with and tone it down", then add rules; the GM can absorb complexity; "Make failures as spectacular as successes". — [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=17311663)
- [Community] [opened] A parent says his kids "hate the complete lack of player agency as there are zero choices to be made" in Chutes and Ladders, but that children love Candy Land despite it being "completely devoid of player agency". (The first pass left out the Candy Land half.) — [Ideonexus, 13 Aug 2018](https://ideonexus.com/2018/08/13/ten-mechanics-to-make-candyland-bearable-or-even-awesome/)

**Research on failure, motivation and probability**
- [Research] [opened] Juul, "Fear of Failing?" (2009): "the most positive players were the ones that failed some, and then completed the game", and "Completing the game without failing was followed by a lower rating". Samples were 9 offline players and 85 self-selected online players; the main effect was p = 0.06. Players who blamed themselves rather than the game differed significantly (p < 0.016). — [Jesper Juul, "Fear of Failing? The Many Meanings of Difficulty in Video Games"](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)
- [Research] [opened] Mueller & Dweck (1998), JPSP 75(1): six studies. "Fifth graders praised for intelligence … After failure, they also displayed less task persistence, less task enjoyment, more low-ability attributions, and worse task performance than children praised for effort." — [Columbia-hosted PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf). Contested: Li & Bates, with 624 children aged 10–12 in three studies, found "Praise for intelligence had no significant effect on cognitive performance" and "no support for mindset-effects on … response to challenge" (SocArXiv working paper 2017; [search extract] published in JEP: General 2019). — [Edinburgh Research Explorer](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/does-growth-mindset-improve-childrens-iq-educational-attainment-o/)
- [Research] [opened] Piaget & Inhelder, *The Origin of the Idea of Chance in Children*: "the concept of probability as a formal set of ideas develops only during the formal operational stage, which occurs about twelve years of age", though younger children intuit long-run outcomes with spinners. — [CAUSEweb summary](https://causeweb.org/cause/node/5908). Green & Ross (1978, *Child Development*), 56 subjects aged 5–17: "considerable agreement with Piaget and Inhelder's description of stage-related verbal features while failing to confirm their description of stage-related nonverbal features". — [CAUSEweb](https://causeweb.org/cause/node/5527)

### Inferences
- **Two layers, not three.** Nielsen's warning rules out the first pass's three-layer plan. Layer 1 (default, everyone): "something happens" outcomes with fixed numbers the bot computes, and one defense number. Layer 2 (opt-in or later): the full defense mix and the momentum spend. NTYE's age-based rule levels are the tabletop precedent.
- **Don't make kids do the miss-band arithmetic.** At 6–8, "math beyond basic addition and subtraction can be difficult". "Missed by ≤ modifier + half proficiency" is fine as a rule the bot applies, but the player should see only "Close! You grazed it" or a momentum pip.
- **Keep real misses, and turn them into a resource.** Juul's data and Laws's argument say failure has value. Kids on Bikes and NTYE show the kid-friendly pattern: a miss earns a token that helps later. That matches direction 1's momentum almost exactly.
- **Frame misses as the foe's defense or the hero's effort, never as the hero being bad.** Edwards ("a miss is not a specific whiffed attempt"), the Angry GM (dodges vs armor) and Mueller & Dweck (effort framing, despite the replication dispute) agree. Pairing it with the defense mix gives a natural line: "The goblin's armor turned your blade, but it looks scared. Try its Will?"
- **Show effects, not percentages.** Formal probability arrives around 12, and Meier's players misread odds even as adults.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of combat rules or miss rates for children aged 8–12 in RPGs. The child-specific guidance is designer and parent practice.
- The Hero Kids, Amazing Tales and Kids on Bikes rulebooks were not opened (paid). Details come from reviews.
- The UCL paper on young children's probability intuitions was blocked (403), and the CAUSEweb research listing (p. 72) was not reopened. Both are removed from findings.

## 7. Combat pacing in play-by-post and asynchronous games

### Takeaway
Practitioner writing agrees that strict turn order and long fights kill play-by-post games. One GM's best-remembered combat "took three weeks", and combat was "one of the biggest reasons why the game ended". The standard fixes are side-based initiative ("Us > Them"), post-by timers (24–48 hours), default actions for absent players, and posting defenses openly so nobody waits on a question. None of these sources measures empty turns, but each empty turn costs one more full posting cycle.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- In play by post, a fight's real-time length is about rounds × the posting cycle. Fewer empty turns shorten fights, as the sim's 0.2–0.3 rounds suggest, and fewer empty turns also mean fewer posting cycles spent on nothing. That makes "something happens on most rolls" worth more on Discord than at a table.
- "Be transparent with ACs" supports showing the defense number (Layer 1) rather than hiding it.
- Momentum that carries across turns has to survive a day's gap and still be legible. Showing it as a pip on the hero's status line fits that better than a modifier the player has to remember.
